# Synthesis of Wage Labor and Capital
A good is something (an object or a service) capable of satisfying a human need. In all societies, human beings must consume goods in order to live and to maintain a good standard of living.

A product is a type of good that is not already available/usable in nature, meaning that it’s the result of human activity, of the purposive processing of some raw materials through certain work tools.

A commodity is a type of good or product that is collected/made to be exchanged for other goods; its purpose is not immediate consumption, but exchange on the market.

Different commodities can satisfy different needs, in fact commodities differ from each other because each has a different utility (use value). All commodities, however, are united by the fact that they are products of human labor, and this gives them a value (if labor is performed under the social conditions of commodity production, under the social division of labour). But if a product fails to satisfy an effective social need, the private labour expended upon it fails to receive social validation (in that case the product is not socially recognised to “hold” value even if it took a lot of work to make, as the value of private labor must be socially validated through exchange).
##
The value of a commodity depends on how much social labor is required for its production. The social labor embodying a commodity represents how much labor the average worker in a given society must employ on average to produce that commodity. The value of a commodity is measured by how many working hours it takes on average to produce it.

The value of a commodity depends on the average level of productivity under the socially prevailing conditions of production, on how developed its productive forces are, that is, on how efficient the tools of work are on average and on how skilled the workers are on average. The value of a commodity does not depend on the skill of the individual worker. A worker who takes longer than average to make his or her commodities does not create commodities of greater value, just as commodities made by a very efficient worker are not less valuable.

If in a society it takes an average of two hours of labor to make a commodity, then in that society this commodity is worth two hours of social labor. If more efficient methods or tools of work that halve the amount of time required for the production of that commodity are discovered, then in this society its value will also be halved.
##
The value of a commodity depends on the value transferred to it both by the raw materials, the tools of labor, and the production plant (accumulated labor) by which it was made according to their rate of consumption, and by the new value created by the living labor of the laborer; this new value is divided into the value reproducing labor-power and the surplus value.

The means of production transfer to the commodity part of the social labor they embody according to how much they are used, but only human labor can create new value (value of a commodity = value transferred from the means of production + value of the reproduction of living labor + surplus value of living labor).

If a hammer worth 1 hour of social work is consumed on average after 600 hammer blows, then in each of the 10 commodities it has worked (with 60 hammer blows each) will be transferred from the value of the hammer 6 additional minutes of social work; similarly, if an average of 1'800 commodities can be produced in a production plant worth 240 hours of social labor before the building is "consumed" and risks collapsing, then an additional 8 minutes of social labor will be transferred into the individual commodities; furthermore, if a worker who works 8 hours contributes to the production of an average of 48 commodities, then an additional 10 minutes of social labour will be incorporated into each commodity.
##
When two people exchange commodities, they are exchanging certain amounts of value. To determine whether a person has obtained an advantageous exchange, one must compare the value of the quantity of goods he has received with the value of the quantity of goods he has surrendered: if he receives a quantity of goods that embodies more social labor than that gave, he has made an advantageous exchange; if he receives one that embodies less, he has made a disadvantageous exchange. In non-monopolized economies, the value of traded commodities tends to be equivalent (in the text we assume to be in a non-monopolized economy).

If the value of two commodities exchanged is equivalent, then the value of the quantity of the first commodity and that of the quantity of the second commodity must be equal to the same quantity of social labor. To facilitate trade, it becomes necessary to quickly express and compare the value of commodities: this function is performed by money (universal equivalent).
##
Price is the monetary expression of value. The price of a commodity is determined by its value but varies according to the relationship between supply and demand, i.e. between how much a commodity is demanded by society and how much of it is available. The price of a commodity is almost never equivalent to its value, and this is because the levels of supply and demand are constantly changing; consequently, the price of commodities always fluctuates either above or below their value. It should be noted, however, that, on average, the periods in which the price of a commodity exceeds its value are balanced by the periods in which its price falls below its value; although commodities are rarely sold at their value, the historical average of the prices of commodities tends to equal their value (this is a simplification of Marx’s theory).

Bourgeois economists tend to confuse the price of a commodity with its value. They tend to believe that the relationship between supply and demand directly determines the value of a commodity; they tend to believe that the price of a commodity is automatically equivalent to its value. They are wrong: supply and demand explain why the market price of a commodity is above or below its value, but they don’t explain its value (its value is explained by the socially necessary labor time needed to produce it). The price of a commodity is equivalent to its value only when demand equals supply (this is another simplification).
##
By further developing the productive forces, men are exposed to the social necessity to change their mode of production, hence to change how they earn their living, and so to change all their social relations. Different economic systems lead to different social systems; different modes of production tend to generate different class structures

In ancient times, the worker was generally a slave directly owned by a master and received only the means of subsistence strictly necessary for his survival. In feudalism the worker was mostly a serf linked to the land, which was owned by a feudal lord; on some days he had to work his feudal lord's field while on others he could cultivate a part of the common land for his own sustenance.

In both cases, only a part of their work was actually “paid”. In the first case, it does not even seem that a part of the work is “paid”, while in the other the “paid” and the “unpaid” work are visibly separated and clearly distinguishable. In every class society, workers necessarily perform labour for their own social reproduction and they also perform surplus labour which is appropriated by the ruling class; the form of this appropriation differs historically (according to the development of the productive forces, the relations of production and the outcomes of class struggle).

Following the industrial revolution and the birth of the world market, the prevailing economic system became the capitalist one and the worker became a wage worker. Even in capitalism only a part of the worker's work is actually paid, but it is not possible to immediately recognize which part is paid and which is unpaid. It seems to the wage worker that all the work he does is paid for because the work is masked by a contract and by the form of the wage he receives at the end of the month (the wage worker thinks the capitalist pays him for his labor, while in reality he pays him for his labor-power).
##
The capitalist system of production is organized to produce mainly commodities with the aim of increasing the value of capital, therefore for the realization of a profit by the owners of capital (capitalists). This economic system incessantly tries to transform all goods and products into commodities (it makes them usable only behind an economic transaction).

In capitalism, a worker, in addition to producing commodities, produces capital. Material products become capital when they are used to increase the value of other material products through the system of wage labor. This is because capital is preserved and multiplied in value only through the use of the labor-power of wage workers.

Capital is the social relation of production of capitalist society. The existence of capital depends on the existence of a class that has capital but not the collective labor-power necessary to increase its value (capitalists) and a class that cannot gain access to the means of subsistence except by selling its labor-power in exchange (wage workers). Capital cannot exist without wage-labor, and universalized wage-labor cannot exist without capital; capitalism is based on the exchange between labor-power and capital.
##
In capitalism, a person who buys a commodity on the market knows neither who produced it nor under what social conditions it was made. The relations of production appear at first sight as simple relations between money, prices and commodities; under the social conditions of commodity production, the social relations between humans necessarily appear in the objective form of a relation between commodities, as if they were relations between things and not between humans (for example, the social relation between capitalist and wage worker appears as a neutral exchange between legal equals - who are actually materially very unequal, and neither of whom can be fully embodied by an abstract "legal agent").

There is the impression that commodities have a value of their own (commodity fetishism), and not that their value is instead a representation of the quantity of social labor embodied in them. In this way, capitalism conceals the historical and social character of exploitation and presents itself as a natural and universal system.
##
In capitalism the working capacity of the worker (labor-power) is a commodity like any other, with the difference that its use tends to generate a value higher than its reproduction costs. The wage-laborer, in order to earn his livelihood, is forced to exchange his labor-power for wages with a capitalist; this is a peculiarity of capitalism.

Like any commodity, the value of labor-power tends to decrease with each development of the productive forces (if the price of the means of subsistence decreases), and since the relation between the supply and demand of labor-power is also constantly changing, the wages of the workers are also exposed to all market fluctuations.

A worker's wage is the price of his labor-power, and it tends to be equivalent to the cost necessary to reproduce the working class; that is, it tends to be equivalent to the cost necessary to keep the wage worker alive as his specific type of wage worker, which also includes the cost of his education and job training, in addition to the cost necessary to allow him to support his family and so to raise future wage workers (these costs are historically and socially determined).

Often individual workers do not receive enough to maintain a good standard of living, but the wages of the entire working class tend to adjust within the limits of their fluctuations to allow enough workers to perpetuate the existence of their class.
##
The wage-laborer is free to work as many hours of his life as he pleases for the capitalist who offers him the most; moreover, he is free to leave him when he wants and vice versa. But the wage-laborer cannot live unless he auctions off enough hours of his life to a capitalist, and he cannot leave the whole capitalist class unless he renounces his own existence. The wage-laborer “belongs” to the whole capitalist class, and it’s up to him the task to find a buyer in this class.

Since the capitalist possesses both the means of production and the labor-power (the wage-laborer is forced to sell it to him) necessary to produce the commodity, the wage-laborer has no right either to possess the product of his labor or to enjoy directly the earning obtained from its sale; the wage-laborer receives from the capitalist only the price of his labor-power (the wage), not the total value which he creates.

The product of the wage-laborer's activity is not the purpose of his activity: life for him begins when his work ceases. The wage worker does not consider the hours he auctions off to the capitalist as an integral part of his life, but as a necessary sacrifice in order to be able to continue living.

And as soon as the wage worker receives the reward of his exploitation, his wage, the landlord (rent), the banks (loans and mortgages), the state (taxes) and the merchants (daily expenses) swoop down on him, as also they want to extort an earning from him.
##
The wage of the wage-laborer cannot be on average higher than the value of the commodities he produces, but the value of the commodities produced by him may well exceed his wage. Wages are not determined by the total value produced by the worker, but by the cost necessary to keep the worker operational. The difference between the value generated by the worker and the wage he receives is the source from which the capitalist derives his profit.

Let's suppose that the commodities that the worker has to consume to keep himself operational cost him a total of 4 hours of social work. In this case, the worker would only need to work an average of 4 hours a day to live (necessary work). But, in order to make a profit, the capitalist must make the working-day last longer than the time it takes for the worker to produce the equivalent of his wage. So let's assume that the capitalist decides to make the working day last 8 hours. In this case, the worker must work 4 extra hours in addition to those necessary to support himself (surplus labor), which generate an extra value (surplus value) that will be appropriated by the capitalist; thus the capitalist, by paying the value of a day’s labor-power (4 hours of social labor), he obtains the full value generated during the working day (in which 8 hours of social labor are embodied), and so obtains a profit (of 4 hours of social labor).
##
When the capitalist sells the commodity at its value, he necessarily sells it at a profit. He not only sells what has cost him an equivalent, but also sells what has cost him “nothing”. The relationship between capitalist and wage worker is exploitative even when the capitalist pays the full value of the labor-power of the wage worker, even when the exchange appears formally fair.

The profit of the capitalist originates in the process of commodity production. Bourgeois economists tend to falsely believe that profit originates in the process of commodity exchange: they tend to start from false premises to arrive at false conclusions. During the process of commodity exchange there’s the realisation of profit, not the creation of profit.

The trend of workers' wages tend to be inversely proportional to the trend of the capitalist's profits: if the workers' wage increases, the capitalist's profit decreases and vice versa (with the increase in profit, wages can also increase but with a lower growth rate than that of profits). Capitalists and wage workers are constantly fighting to increase profits or to raise wages, that is, to define how much of the value generated by the worker will be paid and how much will be appropriated by the capitalist.

The interests of the capitalist class and those of the working class are antagonistic and irreconcilable: in order for the capitalist class to improve its long-term living conditions, it must do so at the expense of the long-term living conditions of the working class and vice versa.

The will of the capitalist class and that of the working class consist in wanting to improve the living conditions of their own class at the expense of those of the other class. But we must not investigate the will of the classes, we must investigate the structural limits that hinder and mediate the execution of their will. We need to understand how class society actually works, not how each class would like it to work.
##
Total profits correspond to the total amount of surplus value squeezed from the wage workers. The rate of surplus-value corresponds to the ratio of total profits to wages. The rate of profit, on the other hand, corresponds to the ratio of total profits to invested capital (surplus value does not all consist of the capitalist profits, but it’s divided into industrial or commercial profits, in interest, and in ground rent; I skip these clarifications to keep things simple).

Suppose the capitalist earns 2'000 € by selling the commodities made by the wage workers. If the capitalist pays 1'000 € in wages, he consequently obtains a surplus-value of 1'000 €, and therefore the rate of surplus-value is 100% (surplus-value/wages = 1). If, on the other hand, we assume that the capitalist still gains 1'000 € of surplus value but also consider that the capitalist paid 4'000 € for the raw materials and the tools of labor, then we would obtain a rate of profit of only 20% (surplus-value/(wages + means of production) = 0.2).

The rate of surplus value is the only measure that indicates the relationship between paid and unpaid labor, that is, the real degree of exploitation of the wage workers. The rate of profit, on the other hand, is the usual measure, and it obscures the real source of surplus value because profit appears to arise from both accumulated and living labor rather than from living labor alone (the value of accumulated labor originates from the value of living labor; accumulated labor does not have a value “magically” on its own; accumulated labor can only transfer its value to the commodity but it cannot create new value).

With the greater automation of the production process, the rate of surplus value tends to increase but the rate of profit tends to decrease. Thanks to innovation, the productivity of the individual wage workers tends to increase and the demand for labor-power tends to decrease, thus, while the costs of new production machines tend to rise, wages tend to fall and the employment rate in automated industries tends to fall.
##
The capitalist entrepreneur must invest part of his profit in the expansion of the productive forces in order to become more competitive in the market. If the capitalist is not competitive enough, his company fails and he becomes a wage worker.

Supposing that, as a result of the expansion of the productive forces, 20% less labor-power is required to produce the same quantity of commodities, then the price of the commodities (hence the cost of living, assuming these are staple commodities) would fall by 20%, and so would the value of labor-power (still assuming they are staple commodities, hence that they are necessary for the working-class social reproduction). Thus the hours of labor that the wage worker spends to produce the equivalent of his daily wage would fall from 4 to 3.2, the hours of surplus labor would rise from 4 to 4.8 and, consequently, the rate of surplus-value would increase from 100% to 150% (4.8/3.2 = 1.5).

In this case, the wage worker’s material conditions would remain unchanged because his wage would fall by 20% (the nominal wage would decrease) and so would the cost of living (the real wage would stay the same). But in this way the material conditions of the wage worker will worsen compared with the material conditions of the capitalist (the relative wage decreases): in this case the capitalist can pay the worker the same real wage as before even if the worker now produces a greater surplus-value for him; the social gap that divides the wage worker from the capitalist increases and the domination of the capitalist class over the working class is strengthened.

The rapid growth of productive capital equals a rapid growth in profits, a rapid decrease in relative wages and a rapid increase in social inequality. To say that "the worker has an interest in the rapid growth of capital" is to say that the more rapidly the worker increases the capitalist's wealth, the greater will be the crumbs that fall on him.
##
Capitalists tend to do everything they can to keep wages low, to minimize business costs, and to extend the length of the working day as much as possible because they increase their profits to the same extent. Wage workers are thus forced to conduct wage struggle to defend their living standards, to be able to work less, with better working conditions and with more decent wages.

To safeguard their profits when an industry becomes unionized, capitalists tend to withdraw their capital from that industry to invest it in a non-unionized industry; and if an entire country were to adopt reforms in favor of the wage workers, the capitalists would try to move their industries en masse to countries where working conditions are worse and where profits are higher (and they would manipulate the politics of the various countries to hinder the unionization of workers).

The capitalists say that it would be useless to raise wages anyway because, according to them, the cost of living would rise accordingly to match the increase in wages. This is false. The price of a commodity is determined by its value, by how much labor is socially necessary for its production, not by the ratio of wages to profits. A general increase in wages lowers the rate of surplus value of the capitalists but it does not proportionally raise the price of commodities.

If anything, an increase in wages would cause an increase in the demand for staple commodities, which would generate a temporary increase in market prices, followed by an increase in supply, which again would cause prices to fall to their former level, if not to an even lower one.
##
A capitalist can beat the competition mainly by selling at lower prices, mainly by reducing the cost of production of his commodities, and therefore mainly by developing the productive forces as much as possible. With the development of the productive forces, the division of labor increases and more and more machinery of better quality is introduced into the workplace.

The more powerful and expensive means of production allow the capitalist to sell his commodities at lower prices, but at the same time they force him to sell more commodities. However, when the competing capitalists also introduce the same machines and the same division of labor, then the new market price will tend to fall below the pre-innovation value of the commodities and will instead gravitate around the post-innovation value.

Thus the capitalists find themselves in the same competitive situation as they were before the introduction of the new means of production, but now they are forced to sell more commodities than before at less than the previous price. At this new point, capitalists must once again compete for market supremacy.
##
In the same measure as the division of labor increases, labor is simplified and the skill of the single worker tends to become superfluous; the more work is simplified, the more competition tends to increase and the more wages tend to fall. The result is that the more the wage worker works, the less he tends to earn; the more he competes with the other wage workers, the more miserable the working conditions of his class tend to become.

Advanced machinery and optimised management tend to replace skilled workers with low-skilled workers and they tend to throw workers out on the streets in ever greater masses as the innovative industry becomes more and more developed and productive, since their employment would no longer be profitable for capital. The existence of a mass of unemployed and precarious workers makes it easy to replace wage workers. This pushes the working class to accept the unfavourable working conditions that the capitalist class imposes on it.

Under capitalism, machinery tends to subordinate workers’ skills to the requirements necessary for the sustainment of the system of wage labor, in which the wage workers class is exploited by the capitalist class; it tends to place human social capacity (both manual and intellectual) under the control of capital.

It is assumed that, if the demand for machinery increases, at least the demand for skilled workers in the machinery industry will also necessarily have to greatly increase. But now the machinery is also applied to the production of cutting-edge machinery. The worker thus becomes the simple operator/administrator of an incredibly complex machine; he becomes a mere appendage of the machine.
##
In the same measure that the capitalists are compelled to exploit the existing productive forces on an ever-increasing scale, so do the crises become more frequent and violent. This is because the more the mass of commodities grows, and therefore the need for extensive markets, the fewer and fewer markets tend to remain to be exploited.

Markets become saturated with commodities that can no longer be sold at a profit; there tends to be an overproduction relative to profitable demand and an underproduction relative to social need. This happens either because everyone already owns that commodity and does not need to buy it back, or because with the development of the productive forces the wages of the workers have been reduced to such an extent that they can no longer afford to buy them, or because they have been fired because their job has been automated (also because of disproportions between industry branches, overaccumulation of capital and excessive credit expansion; these are more advanced clarifications).

The small middle classes fall into the ranks of wage workers because they cannot survive in a struggle in which the first condition for success is production on an ever-increasing scale.

The capitalists, in order to safeguard their profits, overcome crises through the temporary forced destruction of a mass of the productive forces (wage workers are fired and companies are closed) and through the conquest of new markets. This mechanism tends to restore the conditions for the profitability of capital, but it doesn’t abolish the contradictions that generated the crisis. In this way, capitalists prepare for more global and more violent crises, and they reduce the means to prevent future crises.
##
Modern industry has transformed the small workshop of the master craftsman into the great factory of the industrial capitalist. Whole masses of workers are organized as soldiers and are placed under the supervision of a whole hierarchy of officers and sergeants.

The need for ever more extensive trade has given a global scope to the production and consumption of all countries; both in the field of material and intellectual goods. In place of the subsistence economy and national isolation comes omnilateral trade, mutual (but asymmetrical) dependence between nations.

The progress of industry tends to put in place of the isolation of wage workers through competition, their revolutionary union through association. With the development of industry, the very system of wage labor on which the rule of the capitalist class is supported tends to enter into crisis.

The trade unions do a good job of defending the living conditions of the working class against the attacks of capital, but they mostly limit themselves to making the wage slavery of the workers more bearable. It is counterproductive to just demand fair wages, good working conditions and full employment from an economic system that inevitably tends to produce extreme inequalities.

The wage workers of all countries, in addition to organizing themselves to carry on the wage struggle, must organize themselves to overthrow the system of wage labor that keeps them subservient to the capitalist class. They must overthrow the economic system oriented towards the production of profit to establish one oriented towards the direct satisfaction of the social needs of the people.
