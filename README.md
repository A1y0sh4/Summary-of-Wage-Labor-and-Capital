# Summary-of-Wage-Labor-and-Capital
A good is an object capable of satisfying a human need. In all societies, human beings must consume goods in order to live and to maintain a good standard of living.

A product is a type of good that is not already available in nature and that is the result of the human processing of some raw materials through certain work tools.

A commodity is a type of product that is made to be exchanged for other goods; its purpose is not immediate consumption, but exchange on the market.

Different commodities can satisfy different needs, in fact commodities differ from each other because each has a different utility (use value). All commodities, however, are united by the fact that they are products of human labor, and this gives them a value. But a product that does not satisfy a need cannot be a commodity and consequently has no value either (even if it took a lot of work to make).
##
The value of a commodity depends on how much social labor is required for its realization. The social labor embodying a commodity represents how much labor the average worker in a given society must employ on average to produce that commodity. The value of a commodity is measured by how many working hours it takes on average to produce it.

The value of a commodity depends on the average level of productivity of the society in which it is consumed, on how developed its productive forces are, that is, on how efficient the tools of work are on average and on how skilled the workers are on average. The value of a commodity does not depend on the skill of the individual worker. A worker who takes longer than average to make his or her goods does not produce goods of greater value, just as goods made by a very efficient worker are not less valuable.

If in a society it takes an average of two hours of labor to make a commodity, then in that society this commodity is worth two hours of social labor. If more efficient methods or tools of work are discovered in this society that halve the amount of time required for the production of that commodity, then its value will also be halved.
##
The value of a commodity depends on the value transferred to it both by the raw materials, the tools of labor, and the production plant by which it was made according to their rate of consumption, and by the new value created by the living labor of the laborer.

The means of production transfer to the commodity part of the social labor they embody according to how much they are used, but only human labor can create new value.

If a hammer worth 1 hour of social work is consumed after 600 uses, then in each of the 10 commodities it has worked will be embodied 6 additional minutes of social work; similarly, if 1,800 commodities can be produced in a production plant worth 240 hours of social labor before the building is "consumed" and risks collapsing, then an additional 8 minutes of social labor will be incorporated into the individual commodities; Furthermore, if a worker who works 8 hours contributes to the production of 48 commodities, then an additional 10 minutes of social labour will be incorporated into each commodity.
##
When two people exchange commodities, they are exchanging certain amounts of value. To determine whether a person has obtained an advantageous exchange, one must compare the value of the quantity of goods he has received with the value of the quantity of goods he has surrendered: if he receives a quantity of goods that embodies more social labor than that surrendered, he has made an advantageous exchange; if he receives one that embodies less, he has made a disadvantageous exchange. In non-monopolized economies, the value of traded goods tends to be equivalent.

If the value of two commodities exchanged is equivalent, then the value of the quantity of the first commodity and that of the quantity of the second commodity must be equal to the same quantity of social labour. To facilitate trade, it becomes necessary to quickly express and compare the value of commodities: this function is performed by money (universal equivalent). Money is in fact the means that is used to express the price that must be paid in order to take possession of a commodity (i.e., to obtain the right to consume it).
##
Price is the monetary expression of value. The price of a commodity is determined by its value but varies according to the relationship between supply and demand, i.e. between how much commodity is demanded by society and how much is available. The price of a commodity is almost never equivalent to its value, this is because the levels of supply and demand are constantly changing; consequently, the price of commodities always fluctuates either above or below their cost of production (expression of value). It should be noted, however, that, on average, the periods in which the price of a commodity exceeds its cost of production are balanced by the periods in which its price falls below its cost of production; Although commodities are rarely sold at their value, the historical average of a commodity's price tends to equal its value.

Bourgeois economists confuse the price of a commodity with its value. They believe the relationship between supply and demand directly determines the value of a commodity; They believe that the price of a commodity is automatically equivalent to its value. They are wrong: supply and demand explain why the market price of a commodity is above or below its value, but they do not explain its value. The price of a commodity is equivalent to its value only when demand equals supply.
##
By further developing the productive forces, men change their mode of production, change how they earn their living, and so do all their social relations. Different economic systems lead to different social systems.

In ancient times, the worker was generally a slave directly owned by a master and received only the means of subsistence strictly necessary for his survival. In feudalism the worker was mostly a serf linked to the farm, which was owned by a feudal lord; on some days he had to work his feudal lord's field while on others he could cultivate a part of the common land for his own sustenance. In both cases, only a part of their work was actually paid. In the first case, it does not even seem that part of the work is paid, while in the other the unpaid and paid work are visibly separated and clearly distinguishable.

Following the industrial revolution and the birth of the world market, the prevailing economic system became the capitalist one. Even in capitalism only a part of the worker's work is actually paid, but it is not possible to immediately recognize which part is paid and which is unpaid. It seems to the wage worker that all the work he does is paid for because the work is masked by a contract and the form of the salary he receives at the end of the month.
##
The capitalist system of production is organized to produce mainly commodities with the aim of increasing the value of capital, therefore for the realization of a profit by the owners of capital (capitalists). This economic system incessantly tries to transform all goods and products into commodities (it makes them usable only after an economic transaction).

In capitalism, a worker, in addition to producing goods, produces capital. Material products become capital when they are used to increase the value of other material products through the system of wage labour. This is because capital is preserved and multiplied in value only through the use of the labor power of wage workers.

Capital is the social relation of production of capitalist society. The existence of capital depends on the existence of a class that has capital but not the labour-power necessary to exploit it (capitalists) and a class that cannot access the means of subsistence except by selling its labour-power in exchange (wage workers). Capital cannot exist without wage-labour, and wage-labour cannot exist without capital; Capitalism is based on the exchange between labour-power and capital.

In capitalism, a person who buys a commodity on the market knows neither who produced it nor under what social conditions it was made. Thus, the relations of production appear at first sight as simple relations between money, prices and commodities; they seem to be relationships between things instead of relationships between human beings. There is the impression that commodities have a value of their own, and not that their value is instead a representation of the quantity of social labour embodied by them. In this way, capitalism conceals the historical and social character of exploitation and presents itself as a natural and universal system.
##
In capitalism the working capacity of the worker (labour-power) is a commodity like any other, only this is measured with the clock while other goods are measured with the scales. The wage-labourer, in order to earn his livelihood, is obliged to exchange his labour-power for wages with a capitalist; This is a peculiarity of capitalism.

Like any commodity, the value of labour-power decreases with each development of the productive forces, and since the relation between the supply and demand of labour-power is also constantly changing, the wages of the workers are also exposed to all market fluctuations.

A worker's wage is the price of his labor-power, and it is equivalent to the cost necessary to reproduce the working class, that is, it is equivalent to the cost necessary to keep the worker alive as a worker, to which must be added the cost of his education and job training, in addition to the cost necessary to enable him to start a family.

Often individual workers do not receive enough to maintain a good standard of living, but the wages of the entire working class adjust within the limits of their fluctuations to allow enough workers to perpetuate the existence of their class.
##
The wage-labourer is free to work as many hours of his life as he pleases for the capitalist who offers him the most; moreover, he is free to leave it when he wants and vice versa. But the wage-labourer cannot live unless he auctions off enough hours of his life, and he cannot leave the whole capitalist class unless he renounces his own existence. The wage-labourer belongs to the whole capitalist class, and it is up to him to find a buyer in this class.

Since the capitalist possesses both the means of production and the labour-power (the wage-labourer is obliged to sell it to him) necessary to produce the commodity, the wage-labourer has no right either to possess the product of his labour or to enjoy directly the profit obtained from its sale; The wage-labourer receives from the capitalist only the price of his labour-power (wages), not the total value which he realises.

The product of the wage-labourer's activity is not the purpose of his activity: life for him begins when his work ceases. The wage worker does not consider the hours he auctions off to the capitalist as an integral part of his life, but as a necessary sacrifice in order to be able to continue living.

And as soon as the wage worker receives his wages, the landlord (rent), the banks (taxes, loans and mortgages) and the merchants (daily expenses) fall on him, who also want to extort a profit from him.
##
The wages of the wage-labourer cannot be on the average higher than the value of the commodities he produces, but the value of the commodities produced by him may well exceed his wages. Wages are not determined by the total value produced by the worker, but by the cost necessary to keep the worker operational. The difference between the value generated by the worker and the wages he receives is the source from which the capitalist derives his profit.

Suppose that the goods that the worker has to consume to keep himself operational cost him a total of 4 hours of social work. In this case, the worker would only need to work an average of 4 hours a day to live (necessary work). But, in order to make a profit, the capitalist must make the working-day last longer than the time it takes the worker to produce the equivalent of his wages. So let's assume that the capitalist decides to make the working day last 8 hours. In this case, the worker must work 4 hours in addition to those necessary to support himself (surplus labor), which generate an extra value (surplus value) that will be appropriated by the capitalist; Thus the capitalist, by paying the price of 4 hours of social labour, obtains a value in which 8 hours of social labour are embodied, and obtains a profit of 4 hours of social labour.
##
When the capitalist sells the commodity at its value, he necessarily sells it at a profit. He not only sells what has cost him an equivalent, but also sells what has cost him nothing.

The profit of the capitalist originates in the process of commodity production. Bourgeois economists falsely believe that profit originates in the process of commodity exchange: they start from false premises to arrive at false conclusions.

The trend of workers' wages is inversely proportional to the trend of the capitalist's profits: if the workers' wage increases, the capitalist's profit decreases and vice versa. Capitalists and workers are constantly fighting to increase profits or to raise wages, that is, to define how much of the value generated by the worker will be paid and how much will be appropriated by the capitalist.

The interests of the capitalist class and those of the working class are antagonistic and irreconcilable: in order for the capitalist class to improve its living conditions, it must do so at the expense of the living conditions of the working class and vice versa.
##
Profits correspond to the total surplus value realized. The rate of surplus-value corresponds to the ratio of profits to wages. The rate of profit, on the other hand, corresponds to the ratio of profits to invested capital.

Suppose the capitalist makes a profit of €200 by selling the goods made by the worker. If the capitalist pays €100 in wages, he consequently obtains a surplus-value of €100, and therefore the rate of surplus-value is 100% (surplus-value/wages = 1). If, on the other hand, we also consider that the capitalist has paid 400 euros for raw materials and tools of labour, then we will obtain a rate of profit of only 20% (surplus-value/(wages + means of production) = 0.2).

The rate of surplus value is the only measure that indicates the relationship between paid and unpaid labor, that is, the real degree of exploitation of the worker. The rate of profit, on the other hand, is the usual measure, and it is very useful for hiding the real degree of exploitation of the worker.

With the greater automation of the production process, the rate of surplus value tends to increase but the rate of profit tends to decrease. Thanks to innovation, the productivity of the individual worker increases and the demand for labor power decreases, thus, while the costs of new production machines rise, wages fall and the employment rate in automated industries falls.
##
The capitalist entrepreneur must invest part of his profit in the expansion of the productive forces in order to become more competitive in the market. If the capitalist is not competitive enough, his company fails and he becomes a wage worker.

Supposing that, as a result of the expansion of the productive forces, 20 per cent less labour-power is required to produce the same quantity of commodities, then the value of labour-power would fall by 20 per cent and so would the price of the commodity (the cost of living would fall). Thus the hours of labor that the worker spends to produce the equivalent of his daily wage would fall from 4 to 3.2, the hours of surplus labor would rise from 4 to 4.8 and, consequently, the rate of surplus-value would increase from 100% to 150% (4.8/3.2 = 1.5).

In this case, the worker would improve his material conditions because his wage would remain unchanged (the nominal wage would remain the same) while the cost of living would decrease (the real wage would increase). But in this way the material conditions of the worker will worsen compared with the material conditions of the capitalist (the relative wage decreases): in this case the capitalist can pay the worker the same wage as before even if the worker now produces a greater surplus-value for him; The social gap that divides the wage worker from the capitalist increases and the domination of the capitalist class over the working class is strengthened.

The rapid growth of productive capital equals a rapid growth in profits and a rapid decrease in relative wages. To say that "the worker has an interest in the rapid growth of capital" is to say that the more rapidly the worker increases the capitalist's wealth, the greater the crumbs that fall on him.
##
Capitalists tend to do everything they can to keep wages low, to minimize business costs, and to extend the length of the working day as much as possible because they increase their profits to the same extent. Wage workers are thus forced to wage struggle to defend their living standards, to be able to work less, with better working conditions and with more decent wages.

To safeguard their profits when an industry becomes unionized, capitalists withdraw their capital from that industry to invest it in a non-unionized industry; And if an entire country were to adopt reforms in favor of the workers, the capitalists would move their industries en masse to countries where working conditions are worse and where profits are higher.

The capitalists say that it would be useless to raise wages anyway because, according to them, the cost of living would rise accordingly to match the increase in wages. This is false. The price of a commodity is determined by its value, by how much labor is socially necessary for its production, not by the ratio of wages to profits. An increase in wages lowers the rate of surplus value of the capitalists but does not raise the price of commodities in the long run.

If anything, an increase in wages causes an increase in the demand for commodities of staples, which generates a temporary increase in market prices, followed by an increase in supply, which again causes prices to fall to their former level, if not to an even lower one.
##
A capitalist can beat the competition only by selling at lower prices, only by reducing the cost of production of his commodities, and therefore only by developing the productive forces as much as possible. With the development of the productive forces, the division of labor increases and more and more machinery of better quality is introduced into the workplace.

The more powerful and expensive means of production allow the capitalist to sell his commodities at lower prices, but at the same time they force him to sell more commodities. However, when the competing capitalists also introduce the same machines and the same division of labor, then the price of the commodity falls even below the new cost of production.

Thus the capitalists find themselves in the same situation as they were before the introduction of the new means of production, but now they are forced to sell more commodities than before at less than the previous price. At this new point, capitalists must once again compete for market supremacy.
##
In the same measure as the division of labor increases, labor is simplified and the skill of the worker becomes superfluous; The more work is simplified, the more competition increases and the more wages fall. The result is that the more the wage worker works, the less he earns; The more he competes with other wage workers, the more miserable the working conditions of his class become.

Machinery replaces skilled workers with low-skilled workers and throws workers out on the streets in ever greater masses as they become more and more developed and productive.

The existence of a mass of unemployed and precarious workers makes it easy to replace wage workers. This pushes the working class to accept the unfavourable working conditions that the capitalist class imposes on it.

It is assumed that, if the demand for machinery increases, at least the demand for skilled workers in the machinery industry will also necessarily have to increase. But now the machinery is also applied to the production of cutting-edge machinery. The worker thus becomes the simple operator of an incredibly complex machine; he becomes a mere appendage of the machine.
##
In the same measure that the capitalists are compelled to exploit the existing productive forces on an ever-increasing scale, so do the crises. They become more frequent and violent because the more the mass of goods grows, and therefore the need for extensive markets, the more the world market shrinks and the fewer and fewer markets remain to be exploited.

Markets become saturated with goods that can no longer be sold at a profit. This happens either because everyone already owns that commodity and does not need to buy it back, or because with the development of the productive forces the wages of the workers have been reduced to such an extent that they can no longer afford to buy them, or because they have been fired because their work has been automated.

The small middle classes fall into the ranks of wage workers because they cannot survive in a struggle in which the first condition for success is production on an ever-increasing scale.

The bourgeoisie, in order to safeguard its profits, overcomes crises through the forced destruction of a mass of the productive forces (wage workers are fired and companies are closed) and through the conquest of new markets. In this way, it prepares for more global and more violent crises, and reduces the means to prevent future crises.
##
Modern industry has transformed the small workshop of the master craftsman into the great factory of the industrial capitalist. Whole masses of workers are organized as soldiers and are placed under the supervision of a whole hierarchy of officers and non-commissioned officers.

The need for ever more extensive trade has given a global scope to the production and consumption of all countries; both in the field of material and intellectual goods. In place of the subsistence economy and national isolation comes omnilateral trade, mutual dependence between nations.

The progress of industry places in place of the isolation of wage-workers through competition, their revolutionary union through association. With the development of industry, the very system of wage labor on which the rule of the capitalist class is supported enters into crisis.

The trade unions do a good job of defending the living conditions of the working class against the attacks of capital, but they only limit themselves to making the wage slavery of the workers more bearable. It is counterproductive to demand fair wages, good working conditions and full employment from an economic system that inevitably tends to produce extreme inequalities.

The wage workers of all countries, in addition to organizing themselves to carry on the wage struggle, must organize themselves to overthrow the system of wage labor that keeps them subservient to the capitalist class. They must overthrow the economic system oriented towards the production of profit to establish one oriented towards the direct satisfaction of the social needs of the people.
